Pricing Apps for Your First Card Show Table in Australia

Your first table teaches one lesson quickly: you will price more cards in a day than you have in the past year. Someone opens a binder, points at eleven cards and asks what you would pay for the lot. You have about a minute. The vendors who look calm in that moment are not guessing. They have two or three apps set up and a rule for turning what those apps say into an offer.

This guide covers which apps to install, split by whether you sell sports cards or TCG, then goes through each one on its own. It finishes with the part the apps do not do for you: converting a US comp into an Australian buy price.

Pick your kit by what you sell

You do not need every app. You need a fast one, a deep one and eBay.

  • Sports cards: 130 Point for quick comps, Card Ladder for history on bigger cards, eBay sold listings for Australian sales.
  • TCG: Collectr or TCGplayer for raw singles, PriceCharting for graded, eBay sold listings for anything scarce or local.
  • Both: start with 130 Point, Collectr and eBay. Add paid tools once the table is paying for them.

The split exists because the two markets work differently. Sports cards come in dozens of parallels and many trade only a few times a year, so you price from individual sales. TCG singles trade in volume, so a daily market price holds up. Graded TCG cards behave like sports cards and need individual comps.

Sports card apps

130 Point

What it does: searches completed sales across eBay and the major auction houses. Its app listing names Fanatics Collect, Goldin, Heritage, Pristine and MySlabs.

Why vendors rely on it: it shows the accepted price on eBay Best Offer sales. eBay's standard sold view often shows the asking price instead, which makes cards look more valuable than they are. If you buy off inflated comps, you lose money on every card.

Cost: the website search has long been free. The mobile app sells subscriptions, with a Basic annual plan listed at US$99.99 on the US App Store.

Weak spot: results lean on US listings and US dollars. Australian sports barely feature.

Card Ladder

What it does: keeps a sales history per card and charts it, drawing on multiple marketplaces.

Why vendors rely on it: the last sale tells you today's price. The chart tells you whether you are buying into a card on the way down. For anything you would pay a few hundred dollars for, that is worth the extra thirty seconds.

Cost: free to search values and browse daily sales. Card Ladder Pro, which opens the full sales history and collection tracking, is listed at US$19.99 a month or US$199.99 a year.

Weak spot: it is slow for bulk. Do not open it for a five-dollar card.

Market Movers and Alt

Market Movers tracks price movement on popular cards, with paid tiers from US$9.99 a month. Alt is a US marketplace whose app can value a graded card from a photo. Treat both as optional. Try the free versions before paying for either.

TCG apps

Collectr

What it does: scan or search a card and get a current market value, with price history for raw and graded copies. It covers the main games in one place.

Why vendors rely on it: speed. When a seller brings two hundred cards, you need a number per card in seconds. It also doubles as your stock list, so you know what your display case is worth at the start and end of the day.

Cost: free for the basics. Collectr Pro is listed at US$7.99 a month or US$59.99 a year and adds unlimited scanning, export and longer price history. If you scan in volume, you will hit the free limit fast.

Weak spot: values reflect overseas market data. Always sanity-check a scan result against the card in your hand. Scanners misread variants.

TCGplayer

What it does: the largest US marketplace for TCG singles, with a price for every card and condition.

Why vendors rely on it: its Market Price is the figure most of the TCG trade means by "market". It averages recent completed sales. Do not confuse it with the lowest listing, which is one seller's asking price. Decide which one your table uses and say so when you quote a percentage.

Weak spot: US prices, US supply. Cards that are plentiful there can be scarce here, and the reverse.

PriceCharting

What it does: shows raw and graded values in one table, built on US eBay sold prices.

Why vendors rely on it: one glance gives you ungraded, PSA 9 and PSA 10 together. That is handy when a customer asks whether their raw card is worth grading, and when a slab comes across the table.

eBay sold listings and Terapeak

Every vendor needs this one, whatever they sell. Search the card, filter to Sold items, and narrow the item location to Australia when you want local evidence. It costs nothing.

As a seller you get a better tool as well. Product Research in eBay's Seller Hub, still widely called Terapeak, shows the actual sold price even when a Best Offer was accepted, according to eBay's Australian help pages. It also goes back further than the standard sold search. Set it up at home before the show. It is awkward to learn on a phone with a queue at your table.

From US comp to Australian buy price

The apps give you a US sale. Your customer wants an Australian offer. Work through the same four steps every time, and write your own numbers on a card you keep behind the table.

  1. Find the comp. Use three to five recent sales of the exact card: same parallel, same grade, same grading company. Throw out the outlier.
  2. Convert it. At an exchange rate of 65 US cents, for example, a US$100 comp is about AU$154. Check the rate on the morning of the show and round it to something you can do in your head.
  3. Work out your sell price. A card already in Australia saves the buyer international postage and import GST, so local prices often sit above the straight conversion. Australian eBay sold listings show you how far above.
  4. Apply your buy percentage. Offers of 70 to 90 per cent of recent sales are commonly reported in the hobby. Pay toward the top for cards that sell within a week and toward the bottom for cards that will sit. Many vendors offer more in trade than in cash.

Remember what a comp is. It is the gross price on a platform that keeps roughly 13 per cent in fees, before postage. A seller who takes 85 per cent in cash from you today, with no listing, no packing and no risk, is not being short-changed. Say that plainly if you are asked. It is true.

Habits that keep you out of trouble

  • Show the screen. Turn the phone around and let the customer see the comp. It ends most arguments before they begin.
  • Be consistent. Use the same source and the same percentage for everyone. Word travels fast at a show.
  • Price your case before doors open. Look up your own stock at home on wifi and sticker it. Save the phone for buying.
  • Plan for bad reception. Exhibition halls and school gyms are hard on mobile data. Screenshot comps for your best cards and bring a battery pack.
  • Know when the apps cannot help. AFL, NRL and cricket cards have little data anywhere. For those, your own sales records are the best guide you will get.
  • Keep a log. Note what you paid and what it sold for. After three shows you will know your real margins better than any app.
