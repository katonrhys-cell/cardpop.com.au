What Is the Vendor Checking on Their Phone? Pricing Apps at Australian Card Shows

You hand a card across the table. The vendor turns it over, tilts it under the light, then picks up their phone and types for ten seconds. A number comes back. That number decides the offer you get, so it helps to know where it came from.

There is no single price guide for cards in Australia. Vendors pull from a handful of apps, and which ones they open depends almost entirely on what they sell. A sports card dealer and a Pokémon dealer two tables apart can be using completely different tools. This guide covers both camps, then goes through each app on its own so you can check the same figures before you walk up.

The short version

  • Sports card vendors mostly check 130 Point and Card Ladder, with eBay sold listings as the fallback.
  • TCG vendors (Pokémon, One Piece, Magic, Yu-Gi-Oh!, Lorcana) mostly check Collectr or TCGplayer for raw cards, and eBay sold listings or PriceCharting for graded cards.
  • Everyone ends up on eBay sold listings at some point, because that is where most recorded sales happen.
  • Nearly all of these tools report in US dollars from US sales. The vendor is converting to Australian dollars in their head.

Why sports and TCG vendors use different apps

The two halves of the hobby price cards differently because the cards themselves are different.

A modern sports card might exist in forty parallels, some numbered to 10 or fewer. Two copies of the "same" card can be a base version and a gold refractor worth fifty times as much. There is no stable market price for a card that trades twice a year, so sports vendors look at individual recent sales, known as comps, and judge from those. Their apps are built around finding the exact sale.

TCG singles are closer to a commodity. Thousands of copies of the same Charizard change hands every month, so a running market price means something. TCG apps show one figure per card and condition, updated daily. Graded TCG cards are the exception. A PSA 10 behaves like a sports card, and vendors go back to individual sold listings for those.

What sports card vendors use

130 Point

130 Point is the one you will see most at a sports table. It is a search engine for completed sales across eBay and the big auction houses: its app listing names Fanatics Collect, Goldin, Heritage, Pristine and MySlabs alongside eBay. The website search has long been free to use, and the mobile app sells subscription tiers on top.

Its reputation rests on one feature. When a seller on eBay accepts a Best Offer, eBay does not reliably show what the buyer paid. 130 Point shows the accepted amount. A card listed at $500 that sold for an offer of $380 is a $380 comp, and a vendor who knows that will not pay you as though it were $500.

Catch for Australians: results lean heavily on US listings and come back in US dollars.

Card Ladder

Card Ladder keeps a long sales history for each card and draws it as a chart. Where 130 Point answers "what did the last five sell for", Card Ladder answers "is this card going up or down". Vendors use it for higher-value cards, graded rookies in particular, where the trend matters as much as the last sale.

The free tier lets you search card values and browse daily sales. Full history and collection tracking sit behind Card Ladder Pro, listed on the US App Store at US$19.99 a month or US$199.99 a year. Serious dealers pay it. Most collectors do not need to.

Market Movers and Alt

You will see these less often. Market Movers, from Sports Card Investor, tracks price movement across popular cards with paid tiers starting at US$9.99 a month. Alt is a US marketplace with its own valuation model and a tool that prices a graded card from a photo. Both are useful second opinions. Neither has much Australian sales data behind it.

What TCG vendors use

Collectr

Collectr is the app most Pokémon and One Piece vendors have open. It is a portfolio tracker first: scan or search a card, and it returns a current market value with a price history chart for raw and graded versions. Vendors like it because it is fast. One search, one number, next card. That matters when someone has tipped a binder of two hundred cards onto the table.

The basic app is free. Collectr Pro, at US$7.99 a month or US$59.99 a year on the US store, adds unlimited scanning, export and longer price history.

Catch for Australians: the figure reflects overseas market data. It is a fair starting point for a common card and a weaker one for anything scarce locally.

TCGplayer

TCGplayer is the largest US marketplace for TCG singles, and its Market Price is the benchmark many vendors mean when they say "market". Market Price averages recent completed sales of that card in that condition. It is different from the lowest listing, which is only what one seller is asking today. When a vendor quotes a percentage of market, ask which figure they mean.

PriceCharting

PriceCharting is built on US eBay sold prices and lays out raw and graded values side by side: ungraded, PSA 9, PSA 10 and so on. Vendors reach for it when a graded TCG card lands on the table and Collectr's single figure is not enough detail.

The one everybody uses: eBay sold listings

Search a card on eBay, open the filters and tick Sold items. Every app above is, to some degree, a tidier view of this data. It is free, and it is the only source here where you can restrict results to cards that sold from Australia.

Two things trip people up. Asking prices are not sold prices, so ignore anything without the sold filter on. And Best Offer sales are unreliable in the standard view: eBay may show the original asking price rather than what was paid. eBay's own Australian help pages confirm that its Product Research tool (Terapeak, inside Seller Hub) shows the actual sold price even when an offer was accepted. Most collectors use 130 Point for the same job.

The Australian problem with all of these

Almost every number a vendor reads off their phone is a US price in US dollars. Turning it into a fair Australian price takes three adjustments.

  • Currency. A US$100 comp is not a $100 card here. At an exchange rate of 65 US cents, for example, it is about AU$154. Check the day's rate.
  • Landed cost. Buying that card from the US means international postage and GST on top. A card already in Australia can fairly sell for more than the converted comp.
  • Thin local data. AFL, NRL and cricket cards barely appear in US tools. For those, Australian eBay sold listings and local Facebook group sales are the only real evidence, and there may be just two or three sales to go on.

None of this is a trick. A good vendor will show you the screen and explain the conversion. If they will not show you the comp, that tells you something too.

How to use this at a show

  1. Look up your cards before you go. Use the same app the vendor will use: 130 Point for sports, Collectr or TCGplayer for TCG. Screenshot the recent sales.
  2. Match the exact card. Parallel, serial number, grade and grading company all change the price. A PSA 9 comp says nothing about a raw copy.
  3. Use more than one sale. One high sale is an outlier. Three to five recent sales give you a range.
  4. Expect to be offered less than the comp. Vendors need margin to cover table fees, travel and the time a card sits unsold. Offers in the 70 to 90 per cent range of recent sales are commonly reported, higher for cards that sell quickly and lower for slow ones. Trade value is usually better than cash.
  5. Ask what they are comping against. "What are you looking at?" is a normal question. Most vendors are happy to turn the phone around.
